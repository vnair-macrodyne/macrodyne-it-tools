Before this replaces everyone’s home page

The notices below are examples, to show the format. Replace them with real ones first — a home page that opens on a stale example is worse than no home page.

Three addresses to fill: the helpdesk route above, the Uptime link at the foot of the ticker, and every tile in the row at the top still showing amber. Each amber item is an address nobody has given me yet.

Who can post. The noticeboard is one SharePoint list. Anyone given write access to that list can post, and their department shows against the notice — IT is the first poster, not the only one. Decide who those people are before the link goes out, because after it does, silence on the page reads as nothing happening rather than nobody posting.

What belongs here: anything a person needs to know today — something is broken, something is being worked on tonight, a phishing email is going round, a shutdown date is confirmed, a system is back. Short. Two lines is a good length.

Four fields for anything that is down: what is down, why, who it hits, and when it will be back. Write them even when the answer is “we don’t know yet” — an honest no ETA, next update at 11:00 stops the phone ringing, and a blank does not.

What belongs in Uptime instead: what was achieved, what it cost or saved, why it was done. The monthly issue is the story; this page is the wire.

The ticker is the slow half. It carries what is in flight, coming next and recently landed — one short line each, no dates and no figures. It only works if it moves when the work moves: an item that says “in flight” for four months does more harm than leaving it off.

The discipline that makes it worth reading: post the fix as well as the fault. A page that only ever announces problems trains people to dread it. Every “happening now” should end up as a “fixed”, with the time it took — that number is the case for IT, made without anyone having to argue it.
