Feature — Hardware

FY26 refresh

Eight computers, and what each one actually needed

This year's refresh follows what management asked for: keep the fleet current without a capital spike. That starts with working out what's wrong, not with a purchase order.

Eight machines have been dealt with so far this year, and they didn't all need the same thing.

Seven needed replacing. They were failing, or built so that nothing useful could be added to them — no amount of memory fixes a machine that's dying. Those seven were replaced with refurbished units specified to the same requirement, at $12,900 less than the equivalent machines would have cost through our usual supplier.

One needed fixing, not replacing. A laptop in the Automation group, slow enough to be a daily irritation, but the problem was memory and storage rather than age. Memory and an SSD went in and it stayed in service — no new machine, no waiting on delivery, no old one to dispose of.

Refurbished doesn't mean second-hand

The clearest case is a design workstation, and it's worth being precise about what refurbished meant here — because it wasn't a hand-me-down. Our supplier builds machines to a requirement, and has been doing that for a number of companies for over fifteen years. We told him what the work needed. The memory and the graphics card were fitted to match.

That distinction is the whole point. A new machine at this budget arrives with whatever the manufacturer chose to put in it at that price — a fraction of the memory, and whatever graphics came built into the processor. This one was specified. The chassis was chosen because it had the room, and the parts went in to suit the work it was going to do.

It carries a one-year warranty, which is the question everyone asks second and nobody asks first.

The designer ended up with a machine that holds a full assembly open and turns it without complaint, for $1,250.

And where we don't yet know

The design team has a question we can't answer from a specification sheet: how much machine does a large assembly actually need? So Dell have lent us one — a high-powered workstation with 64 GB of memory and a 16 GB graphics card — for the mechanical design team to run for sixty days. No cost, no commitment.

It is a useful comparison because the refurbished machine above is a Dell too. Same memory, same maker; a bigger graphics card and a faster processor. Whatever difference the design team notice is that difference, and nothing else.

We've agreed in advance what a good result looks like: files up to 100 MB opening within a couple of minutes, models that move without lag, quick local saves — and all of it while ETO, Teams, Kronos, Outlook and a browser full of tabs are running, because that's the real state of anyone's machine. We'll report what it found when the trial ends.

Where that leaves us

Eight computers dealt with so far this year and $12,900 that stayed in the budget, with the refresh still running. The approach is the same each time: work out what the job actually needs, then find the least expensive honest way to meet it. Sometimes that's a new machine. Often it's a refurbished one. Surprisingly often it's just more memory in the computer already on the desk — and sometimes it's borrowing one for sixty days before deciding anything at all.

If your computer is slow, log it. It may be a twenty-minute fix.

Feature — Systems

Live and available

Two things you can go and use today

Both of these are online now. Neither needs anything installed.

Project Console

Four dashboards and fifteen reports that build themselves from live project data — earning, cost performance, material run-out, inventory coverage, purchase order exceptions, non-conformance costs. The figures update on their own, so what you are looking at is what is true this morning rather than what someone exported last week.

The part worth knowing: the derived numbers are now calculated in one place, the same way for everyone. Margin, percentage complete, earned value and run-out used to be worked out in separate spreadsheets by different people. Purchasing, Quality, Project Management and Finance are now reading the same figures — which means a meeting can be about what to do rather than about whose number is right.

New — Website management

Now in-house

The website is now ours to run

Tree Frog built macrodynepress.com and the site stays as they built it. What's changed is who keeps it running: the work the agency used to do is now done in-house. The defence section of the site remains with Tree Frog.

Aditya Kumar is the prime on this effort, under my supervision — keeping the site healthy, and building the tools the Business Development team use to create their own content. That second part is the more useful half. Rather than routing every page and update through an agency queue, the people who know what needs saying can publish it themselves.

Business Development own what goes on the site. We look after what it runs on.

Security

Audit and access

Who needs access to what

An independent security review of the company's computers was carried out earlier this year. It found things worth fixing, as these reviews generally do, and those findings are being worked through in order of priority.

Two of them are already closed, and you will have noticed one of them. Everyone was required to change their domain password — that was this. It closed off passwords that were weak, or that had been used in more than one place. The second is quieter: company computers now run monitored security software, which watches for the behaviour of an attack rather than only blocking threats it already recognises, and escalates anything suspicious to people who investigate it.

The largest piece of that work isn't a piece of software — it's a question. Who actually needs access to what? Over time, and in every company, access accumulates: people change roles and keep what they had, folders get opened up to solve a deadline and never get closed again, and eventually a great many people can reach a great many things they have no need for.

We're working through that now with our IT partner, defining access by role rather than by history — what a buyer needs, what a designer needs, what someone in Quality needs. Point fixes close individual gaps. Defining roles stops them reopening.

What this means for you: at some point over the coming months, some people will find their access to certain folders or systems has changed. That will be deliberate, not a fault. If something you genuinely need for your work stops being available, tell us and we'll put it back — the goal is accurate access, not less of it.
